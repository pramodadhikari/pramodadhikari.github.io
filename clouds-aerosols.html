Aerosols, clouds, and Himalayan hydroclimate

My doctoral research with John F. Mejia at the University of Nevada, Reno combined satellite observations, reanalysis, and 3 km WRF-Chem simulations to investigate how natural and anthropogenic aerosols influence precipitation over the central Himalayas.

The work connects cloud microphysics with the elevation-dependent rainfall and freezing-level changes that matter for mountain water resources.

ATMOSPHERIC CHEMISTRY AND PHYSICS / 2023

Aerosol effects change with elevation

Using cloud-resolving WRF-Chem simulations of the first monsoonal month of 2013, we isolated aerosol effects through cloud and radiation interactions. Below 2,000 m, aerosols increased heavy precipitation by 12% while reducing light precipitation by 19%. Above 2,000 m, mean precipitation decreased by 11%.

Why it matters: The contrasting response shows why a single regional average can miss important changes in mountain precipitation. The results describe this modeled monsoon period, rather than a universal response for every season.

CLIMATE DYNAMICS / 2021

Cloud development and the freezing level

Our observational analysis found that higher aerosol concentrations were associated with deeper cloud development and greater precipitation during the Indian summer monsoon. The freezing level was about 137 ± 19 m higher on polluted days than on relatively clean days.

Why it matters: Changes in freezing-level height can affect the rain–snow transition and Himalayan hydroclimate. The analysis examined meteorological influences on these associations across daily to interannual timescales.

ATMOSPHERIC ENVIRONMENT: X / 2022

Remote dust influences mountain rainfall

For a June 2018 Thar Desert dust-transport event, we compared 3 km WRF-Chem simulations with and without incoming dust. Including transported dust increased precipitation by 9.6% over terrain between 500 and 3,000 m, alongside changes in cloud droplets and convection.

Why it matters: Dust from distant sources can influence precipitation on Himalayan slopes through aerosol–cloud interactions. This was a single-event sensitivity study, with model biases, so the result should not be interpreted as a long-term regional average.
